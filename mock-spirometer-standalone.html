Mock Spirometer: Continuous Severity & Bronchodilator Challenge

Dial in airway obstruction and restriction independently — including mixed patterns — run the forced exhalation maneuver, and compare repeated trials. Then head to the Bronchodilator Challenge to test whether an obstruction is reversible.

Standalone study tool — extends LO9 (Respiratory): ventilation mechanics, compliance/elastic recoil, and measures of respiratory function.

1 · How the test works

1
Breathe in fully
Inhale maximally, all the way to Total Lung Capacity (TLC)
→
2
Blast out
Exhale as hard, fast, and completely as possible into the mouthpiece — no hesitation
→
3
Keep going
Continue exhaling until volume stops changing (a true plateau), down to Residual Volume (RV)

This is called the forced vital capacity (FVC) maneuver. Throughout the maneuver, the spirometer continuously measures exhaled volume and flow — that's exactly what's plotted in the Volume–Time Trace and Flow–Volume Loop below.

Why technique matters: spirometry is effort- and technique-dependent. A test is only trustworthy if the patient took a truly maximal breath in, started exhaling immediately with no hesitation, and kept exhaling long enough to reach a genuine plateau. Because of this, a clinician has the patient repeat the maneuver a few times and reports the best acceptable, reproducible effort — that's exactly what Section 5 below lets you practice.

6 · Why it looks this way

Bronchodilator Reversibility Challenge

Give this patient an inhaled bronchodilator (e.g., albuterol) and re-test. A rise in FEV1 of ≥12% and ≥200 mL counts as significant reversibility — the classic clue that points toward asthma rather than fixed airway damage like COPD. Restriction doesn't respond to a bronchodilator at all, since it isn't an airway-resistance problem.
